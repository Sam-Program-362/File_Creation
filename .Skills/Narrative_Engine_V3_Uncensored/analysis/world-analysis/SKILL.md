# World Analysis

## Purpose

Understand the setting that the concept actually needs. Identify established world facts, relevant systems, constraints, gaps and narrative implications. Do not build an encyclopedia or invent a conventional fantasy world when the project needs only a small, grounded setting.

## Activation

Use when a setting, location, society, institution, world mechanism, magic/technology system or other environmental structure materially affects the project.

## Procedure

### 1. Establish world scope

Determine whether the project needs:
- a single scene location;
- a small set of recurring locations;
- a town, institution, region or contained setting;
- a broad open world;
- a universe or large-scale setting;
- an abstract, simulated, alternate, virtual or otherwise non-conventional environment.

Use the narrowest scope that fits the user's concept. Do not assume a large map or extensive history is needed.

### 2. Inventory established world facts

Identify whichever of the following are actually present:
- spatial layout, geography and environmental conditions;
- locations, buildings, regions and travel relationships;
- history, eras, timeline and important events;
- societies, cultures, customs, languages and social expectations;
- governments, laws, institutions, organizations and power structures;
- factions, communities, species and populations;
- technology, magic, science, supernatural mechanics or other systems;
- resources, economy, infrastructure and daily-life conditions;
- physics, metaphysical rules, limitations and exceptions;
- atmosphere, aesthetic and environmental identity.

This is an inventory, not a compulsory section list. Analyze only what the concept establishes or needs.

### 3. Identify governing rules and constraints

For each important world system, determine:
- what the rule says;
- where and to whom it applies;
- what causes it to activate or matter;
- its limits and exceptions;
- its consequences;
- what is explicitly prohibited or impossible;
- whether the rule is objective, disputed, unknown or merely believed by an in-world character.

Preserve exact numerical limits, special conditions and exceptions. Do not simplify a rule in a way that changes its behavior.

### 4. Map relationships between systems

Analyze dependencies where they matter. Examples:
- how law relates to government or social class;
- how resources affect travel, trade or daily life;
- how magic or technology changes institutions;
- how geography constrains movement;
- how a world rule affects character abilities and plot possibilities.

Do not invent causal relationships simply because two systems coexist.

### 5. Analyze narrative function

Determine why each important world element matters to the project:
- does it create the central conflict?
- does it constrain or enable characters?
- does it establish atmosphere?
- does it provide discovery or exploration?
- does it shape relationships or social behavior?
- is it necessary context for the downstream narrator?

Prioritize elements that support the concept's narrative gravity. Avoid unrelated lore expansion.

### 6. Handle locations and references

For image or written location references, follow the Reference Analysis router and applicable tags. Separate permanent setting elements from temporary weather, props, lighting, scene composition or a one-time event. Do not assume a single reference shows the entire world.

### 7. Identify consistency and missing structure

Check for:
- incompatible rules or exceptions;
- timeline conflicts;
- geographic contradictions;
- institutions with unclear jurisdiction or function;
- resource or capability limits that have no established basis;
- unexplained consequences that materially affect the premise.

Do not “fix” a contradiction by silently inventing lore. Preserve and flag it when material.

Without Improvise, leave nonessential unknowns open and identify important gaps. With Improvise, complete necessary connective world structure consistent with explicit facts and Bedrocks. Do not include a Missing Elements section when Improvise is active.

### 8. Capture world Bedrocks and tags

Preserve world- and project-level Bedrocks with their exact scope. Apply World Reference, Scenario Reference, Open World, Focus, Exclude, Improvise and Detailed where relevant. Do not apply Open World merely because the project contains more than one location; it means the setting is not intended to be restricted to a closed set of places.

## Output to coordinator

Return:
- appropriate world scope;
- established setting facts and systems;
- important rules, limits and exceptions;
- relationships between systems, where supported;
- locations and environmental details relevant to the concept;
- narrative function and priority;
- contradictions, gaps and unknowns;
- world-scoped tags and Bedrocks;
- information that must later appear in the World Sheet, Context Sheet, Description, Scenario or Lorebook Plan.

Do not generate the final World Sheet here.

## Constraints

- Do not overbuild the world beyond the concept's needs.
- Do not mistake character beliefs, legends or rumors for objective world facts.
- Do not invent history, laws, factions, cultures or mechanisms without support or authorization.
- Do not ignore exceptions to a world rule.
- Do not create empty conventional sections.
- Do not make the initial location the only possible place unless the concept establishes that boundary.
- Do not write a scenario opening or perform live world simulation.

## Completion check

- [ ] Scope fits the project's actual need.
- [ ] Established facts are separated from beliefs and proposals.
- [ ] Rules retain their conditions, limits and exceptions.
- [ ] System relationships are supported, not assumed.
- [ ] World elements are prioritized by narrative relevance.
- [ ] Reference details are interpreted according to purpose.
- [ ] Bedrocks and tags retain scope.
- [ ] Downstream destinations are identified.
